                                                                   1. The game Bulgarian solitaire

                                             The following popular mathematical card game, popularized (but not intro-
                                           duced) by blast Martin Gardner in 1983 [17], is called Bulgarian solitaire.

                                             The game Bulgarian solitaire. Initially, we are given N cards disposed in
                                           several piles. A move consists of removing exactly one card from each pile and
                                           forming a new pile. The operation is repeated over and over.

                                              If the number of cards N is a triangular number, i.e., N is of the form N =
                                           1 + 2 + · · · + k = k(k + 1)/2 for some positive integer k, a remarkable fact is
                                           that, starting from any initial configuration, after a finite number of moves the
                                           Bulgarian solitaire will reach the stable configuration formed by piles of sizes
                                           1, 2, . . . , k. This result was proved in 1982 by Brandt ([9], the assertion after

   It was also considered in [9] the case when the number of cards is not triangu-
lar. Since a deck has only finitely many layouts, the game of Bulgarian solitaire
must cycle. Brandt characterized and counted all cycles for any given deck size
[9, Theorem 5].
   A survey of the earlier history of the game Bulgarian solitaire, including its
name and summarizing subsequent research, was presented in 2012 by B. Hop-
kins [21]. Hopkins pointed out that this game was popularized by M. Gardner
in 1983 [17] with the unusual name Bulgarian solitaire.
   However, in [21] Hopkins reported that this game was discovered in 1980 by
Konstantin Oskolkov by demonstrating the particular case of the game Bulgarian
solitaire concerning five piles with heights 3, 1, 4, 1, 6, namely, he wrote:
   “Around 1980, Konstantin Oskolkov of the Steklov Mathematical Institute in
Moscow traveled by train to give a talk in Leningrad (now Saint Petesburg ). A
man on the train told him of the problem, although the details of the dialog above
are fictional. Oskolkov shared this with his colleagues at the institute; reportedly
when one number theorist heard about it “his face a Satanic expression”, he ran
to his office, closed the door and did not come out until he solved the problem”.
   Notice that problem about BS firstly appeared in 1981 in the famous Russian
journal Kvant [40]. Andrei Toom, a research scientist at Moscow State Univer-
sity, published a related solution in 1981 [40]. Notice that his proof was also
included in 1981 Book on Mathematical Olympiads [41]. In the same year, the
solution of BS was published by B. Bojanov in a Bulgarian high school math-
ematics journal [8] (here BS was defined in the context of heaps of balls) (see
[12]). The solution of BS was also published in 1981 by Eriksson in Sweden
mathematical journal [13]. Hopkins [21] noticed that the three 1981 solutions
of BS use very similar ideas to show that, for n = Tk there is indeeed just the
single fixed point τk = (k, k − 1, . . . , 2, 1).
   Another two proofs about Bulgarian solitaire were established in 1982 by
Brandt [9] (a proof in the setting of integer partitions), and in 1985 by Akin and
Davis [1]. An induction proof of a result about Bulgarian solitaire was given
in 2010 by the author of this paper [32]. Using Pólya enumeration theory (see
[7]), Brandt [9] derived the related formula. Another combinatorial proof of this
formula was given by the author of of this paper in [33].
   D. Knuth [20] started a fall 1982 programming and problem-solving seminar
with BS. In 1983 blast M. Gardner [17] popularized BS in his mathematics col-
umn from Scientific American. D. Knuth has also conjectured that for triangular
number N the length of the game (i.e., the number of moves before the final
position is reached) is at most k(k − 1). This conjecture was solved affirmatevly
in 1985 by K. Igusa [30]. The proof of a generalization of this conjecture for

2. A formal approach to the mathemacical card game Bulgarian
                            solitaire
   The foundations of the theory of integer partitions were laid by Leonhard
Euler. A goood introduction to this subject are the books [2] and [3]. Let
us now define the game formally. Let N be a positive integer and let λ be a
partition of N having l parts written (λ1 , λ2 , . . . , λl ) in non-increasing order; that
is, N = λ1 + λ2 + · · · + λl with positive integers λ1 ≥ λ2 ≥ . . . ≥ λl ≥ 1. Define
T (λ) as the partition of n with parts λ1 − 1, λ2 − 1, . . . , λl − 1, l, ignoring any
zeros that might occur. So T i (λ) (i = 1, 2, . . .) denotes the partition obtained
by successively applying the shift operation T to λ a total of i times.
   Starting with a partition λ, we describe Bulgarian solitaire by repeatedly
applying the shift operation to obtain the sequence of partitions
                                  λ, T (λ), T 2 (λ), . . . .
We say a partition µ of N is T -cyclic if T i (µ) = µ for some i ≥ 1.
  If N is arbitrary, Brandt noted that repeated application of T leads into a
cycle of partitions, since there are only a finite number of these. Furthermore, a
                                                                                  4

cycle of partitions is completely determined by the sequence of the consecutive
lengths of the partitions in the cycle. Motivated by this fact, Brandt ([9, p.
483][2] defines the set Mn by

(1)                     Mn = {σ = (σi )i∈Z : max σi = n,
                        where for all i, σi = |{σj |j < i, σj ≥ i − j }|},

where |S| denotes the cardinality of a set S. If σ ∈ Mn , then by Proposition 2
in [9], σi ∈ {n, n − 1} for all i ∈ Z. As an easy consequence of this fact, Brandt
(cf. proof of Theorem 5 in [9]; ]also see [1], Theorems 4 and 5, [18], Theorem 2.1
and Etienne [16]), characterized all T -cyclic partitions for an arbitrary positive
integer N . This result is given as follows.
Theorem.[16] Let N = 1 + 2 + · · · + k + r, 0 ≤ r ≤ k. Then a partition λ of N
is T -cyclic if and only if λ has the form
                      (k + δk , k − 1 + δk−1 , . . . , 1 + δ1 , δ0 ),
where each δi is 0 or 1 and ki=0 δi = r.

   In particular (see the assertion after the proof of Theorem 4 in [9]). For a

Corollary (Brandt’s Equilibrium Theorem) If N = 1 + 2 + · · · + k, then (k, k −
1, . . . , 1) is the unique T -cyclic partition of N .
  Recall that the above theorem follows from Theorem 4 in [1] whose proof is
based on Brandt’s result. Theorem 5 in [1] which is proved directly, also gives a
description of all T -cyclic
  partitions for arbitrary N as in above theorem. The above corollary is proved
by Etienne [16] by introducing a natural array representation of a partition λ.
The idea in his proof is applied in the proof of Theorem 2.1 in [18] (the above
theorem) to general N .

3. Some variations and new variants of the card game Bulgarian
                             solitaire
  For information about the earlier history of the game Bulgarian solitaire and
a summary of subsequent research, see reviews by Hopkins [21] and Drensky
[12].
  Many variants of Bulgarian solitaire have been suggested in the literarure (see
[12] for an extensive survey,) such as in 1985 Austrian solitaire by E. Akin and
M. Davis [1] (also see [4]), in 1992 Montreal solitaire by C. Cannings and J. Haigh
(England) [10], in 1995 by R. Servedio and Y.N. Yeh [39], in 1995 (without name)
by Y.N. Yeh, in 1997 Carolina solitaire by Andrej Andreev (Bulgaria) [31]),
                                                                                   5

[10], in 2003 Random Bulgarian solitaire by Popov [37], in 2004 Two-handred
Bulgarian solitaire by Tim Bancroff [31] and in 2012 (the game without name) by
B. Hopkins [15]. The PhD thesis [15] deals with processes on integer partitions
and their limit shapes, with focus on deterministic and stochactic variants on
Bulgarian solitaire. In 2016 Olson [38] presented a generalization of Bulgarian
solitaire, the so-called σ-Bulgarian solitaire, in which multiple cards may be
picked from a single pile. For another generalization of Bulgarian solitaire, see
[14].
  Bulgarian solitaire and its variants are extensively researched in Combinatorial
Game Theory. S. Dorée (of Augsburg College, Minneapolis, USA) called BS “a
somewhat distant relative of two-player African pebble games Mancala” (see [31]
and [11]).
  In [25] Harris and Nguyen introduced a new representation of Bulgarian soli-
taire that is convenient for the study of related generating functions (also see
[23]). In [25] it was also proved two instances of Pham’s conjecture [36].

                                 4. Conclusion
   In this article, we present a survey of variations of Bulgarian solitaire game.
All these games are inspired by the popular mathematical card game Bulgarian
solitaire. The popularity of the Bulgarian solitaire started around 1980. Note
that in 1983 the paper [17] by Martin Gardner was the starting point of the
popularity of the Bulgarian solitaire among mathematicians all over the world
(see [12]).
